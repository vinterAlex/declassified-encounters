The Nixon file

There is no 1960 treaty. There is a paper trail of where the story came from.

The most durable claim in UFO culture is that Nixon signed an agreement with aliens around 1960. No treaty text, signing photograph, Senate record, NSC memorandum or presidential library document exists. What exists is three unrelated legends that merged in the 1980s and 1990s.

No primary document

The three claims, and their first appearance

  • 1954
    Greada Treaty     Eisenhower signs a pact with greysFirst appears in William Cooper's 1989 lectures — 35 years after the alleged signing. No text, no venue, no witnesses.
  • 1957–1960
    Valiant Thor     A Venusian lives at the Pentagon and meets NixonFrom Frank Stranges' self-published 1967 book Stranger at the Pentagon. No Pentagon, Secret Service or personnel record.
  • 1973
    Jackie Gleason     Nixon shows Gleason alien bodies in FloridaA single-source anecdote from Gleason's ex-wife, told years after their divorce. No White House or military log.

The “1960” date comes from the Valiant Thor timeline, not from any agreement.

How to test it in five minutes

  • Ask for the record group and box number. There isn't one.
  • Search the Nixon, Eisenhower and Truman library catalogues. Nothing.
  • Check the NSC meeting minutes for the alleged date. No meeting.
  • Read NARA's MJ-12 forensics: wrong paper, wrong watermark, wrong filing number.
  • Notice that every version names a different date, location and species.

Sources & storage

Everything indexed, and what it costs to keep.

The sizes below are the labels published by the issuing agencies. PURSUE is the only programme that ships bulk ZIP bundles; the rest are archives you download file by file. If you want a local mirror, budget for the documents first — the video is what makes it expensive.

PURSUE bundles — direct downloads

Open war.gov/UFO ↗

Free, direct, no account. Click any size to download the official ZIP.

Sizes are decimal (1 GB = 1,000 MB) and copied from the official download buttons. ZIPs expand by roughly 10–25% when extracted, so a 19.8 GB download needs about 25 GB free. Video dominates: documents alone are only ~5 GB.

One release

curl -L -O \
  "https://www.war.gov/medialink/ufo/release-07/\
documents_release_07_oct_23_2026.zip"

Start with R07 (325 MB). It is the smallest full tranche.

Resumable / parallel

aria2c -x 8 -s 8 -c \
  -d pursue \
  "https://www.war.gov/medialink/ufo/..."

-c resumes after a dropped connection. Large .gov downloads do drop.

Check before committing

unzip -l bundle.zip | head -40
df -h .
du -sh pursue/

List the archive first. Some bundles are mostly repeated reference papers.

Who's who in the paperwork

The acronyms, decoded.

CIA, NSA, NARA, DoW, CNES, FAB — every code in the filter menu, what the agency actually does, and why it appears in this index. Hover a record's agency column or open its drawer to see the same mapping.

Not every acronym is a spy agency. NARA and TNA are archives, CNES is a space agency, FAB and NZDF are military services, and NCAS is an independent sceptics group that reproduced a government report. The badge on each record tells you which kind of source you are reading.

Read the badge before the headline

Four levels of trust

“Declassified” does not mean “true”, and a scan on a blog is not a government release. Every record is tagged with one of these.

Official government

Hosted by the issuing agency. A .gov, .mil or national archive domain. The document is in official custody. It still only proves the document exists.

Institutional

Released by a public institution. A space agency, national archive or university reproduction of an official report. Reliable custody, not necessarily a government portal.

Mirror / partial

Real material, imperfect chain of custody. A release mirrored on the Internet Archive, or a partial activist scan. Usable, but cite the original series reference.

No primary document

A claim, not a record. Included so you can see the myth next to the evidence that contradicts it. Never cite these as sources.

01

Find the file ID. Blue Book uses microfilm roll numbers; PURSUE uses DOW-UAP-D### identifiers. A claim without an ID is a rumour.

02

Open the issuing agency. If the only copy is on a video channel or a PDF aggregator, treat it as unverified until you find the archive record.

03

Read the caution, not the caption. “Unresolved” means insufficient data. “Unidentified” is a filing category, not a conclusion.
